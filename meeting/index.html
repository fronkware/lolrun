          Just for laughs: a joke way to learn how fast zoo animals travel. It isn't safety advice, and no animals are actually loose.

          How it all works: speeds, chases and the code behind it

          Two speeds per animal

          Each animal sprints at top speed for as long as it can manage, then settles into a pace it can keep for hours. A cheetah does 64 mph for about 25 seconds, then walks. A wolf only sprints for 20 seconds but trots at 5 mph all day. Cats win up close; dogs, elephants and ostriches win over distance. During a meeting they chase wherever you are, so you can run away at 6 mph, a 10-minute mile. Every 5 seconds of running (meeting time, so speed-ups count) carries a 1 in 200 chance, which makes a hit about 83% likely if you run a whole 30-minute meeting, of getting hit by one of 20 tall-hooded pickups and SUVs (a lifted F-150, a Ram 2500, a Suburban…). You can keep running, but each hit halves your speed.

          Wildcards

          Rolled fresh each time you start. 🕊️ Pigeons (25%) if you're within 25 miles of a city of 300,000 people or more; they fly in from downtown at 45 mph. 🦈 A shark (10%) if you're within 7.5 miles of the ocean: 30 minutes to get an Uber, load it and set up a temporary tank, then 30 mph, and the tank lasts 15 minutes. 🐝 Bees (4%) from a hive up to 3 miles away, at 15 mph. 🥥 A coconut (1%) lands on you at minute 5, wherever you are. Twelve oddballs (a kid with a bug, a goose, your boss…) at 1% each, and your state's local wildlife shows up 1 to 2 hours in.

          The safety score

          Every arrival before your meeting ends adds danger, weighted by how deadly it is and how much of the meeting it's there for. A lion at minute 5 hurts the score far more than a gorilla at minute 55. 100 means nothing arrives in time.

          What it doesn't model

          Fences, rivers, terrain and traffic. Animals that would rather stay home. Nobody crosses into Alaska or Hawaii, and Hawaii's zoo animals stay on Oahu. You run cross-country in a straight line; only the animals use roads. It's a fun upper bound, not a risk model.

          The data

          A hand-built starter set of zoos, sanctuaries and wildlife parks, with one entry per species at each place. Collections change often, so treat it as approximate.

          Under the hood

          Everything runs in your browser. One HTML page with D3 for the map, plus five static JSON files: zoos and species, US state shapes (us-atlas), big cities, a coastline index, and a road graph. No server, no account, no tracking. The only thing saved is your daily-game history, in your own browser's local storage.

          Location. On load the page asks GeoJS (falling back to ipapi.co) for a rough spot based on your internet address. It's often a few miles off, and if neither answers it starts in San Diego. Search, "Use my location" (your device's GPS, only when you tap it) or tapping the map replace it. The page never uploads your location. A result you copy and share can include it in the link.

          Arrival times. For each animal the page takes the shorter of two routes: a straight line, or a road route. Roads come from Natural Earth's major US highways, about 39,000 points. A shortest-path search (Dijkstra) runs once outward from you over that network, with road miles costing 0.8 of a regular mile because animals go 25% faster on pavement. Each animal can cut up to 10 mi cross-country to reach a road and again to reach you. Time is the sprint first, then the cruise speed for the rest.

          The chase. A meeting steps the clock forward in small slices. Every animal moves its allowed distance along its road path, then straight at wherever you are right now. They react to where you are, not where you're going. Speed chips just compress the same clock.

          Run this way. The 🏃 Run button tries 36 directions, 10° apart. For each it simulates you running at 6 mph in a straight line (stopping at the coast or a border) while everything that's coming chases you live, using the same engine as a meeting. It picks the direction with the best safety score, breaking ties by the latest first arrival, and compares that with standing still. If nothing beats staying put by a minute, it says so. It's a single snapshot: once you move, tap again.

          Randomness. Wildcards are rolled with your browser's random number generator each time a meeting starts or you tap the die, so two meetings in the same spot can differ. Local wildlife comes from a per-state table found by testing which state shape contains your point. The ocean creature you get for a pin in the water comes from a hash of its coordinates, so the same spot always gives the same creature. The daily game is seeded by today's date, so everyone gets the same city and the same falling order.

          Compass. On phones, the compass button asks for device-orientation permission (iOS requires a tap for this). The arrow angle is the true bearing from you to the animal minus your phone's heading. Without a compass the arrows are north-up.

          Heat map. Each pixel gets its own straight-line score from nearby facilities, drawn to a canvas clipped to the US. Near a highway the distance is shrunk so danger stretches up to 25% further, fading smoothly to nothing 8 mi from the road. It's an approximation; your own spot uses the full road routing. Yellow means only local wildlife can reach you.

          Sound. Off until you tap the speaker. Beeps are synthesized with the Web Audio API, and animal voices use your browser's built-in speech, only when you tap.
